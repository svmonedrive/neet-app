In prokaryotes, ribosomes are associated with the plasma membrane of the cell. They are about 15 nm by 20 nm in size and are made of two subunits - 50S and 30S units which when present together form 70S prokaryotic ribosomes. Ribosomes are the site of protein synthesis. Several ribosomes may attach to a single mRNA and form a chain called polyribosomes or polysome. The ribosomes of a polysome translate the mRNA into proteins.

<b>Inclusion bodies:</b> Reserve material in prokaryotic cells are stored in the cytoplasm in the form of inclusion bodies. These are not bound by any membrane system and lie free in the cytoplasm, e.g., <b>phosphate granules</b>, <b>cyanophycean granules </b>and <b>glycogen granules</b>. <b>Gas vacuoles </b>are found in blue green and purple and green photosynthetic bacteria.

<callout icon="💡" color="gray_bg">
Think of ribosomes as automated assembly lines. Their sole purpose is to read genetic instructions and assemble amino acids into functional proteins. Without them, a cell cannot build enzymes, structure, or tools to survive.
</callout>

#### 1. Location: Tied to the Membrane:

In complex cells (like human cells), ribosomes float freely or bind to an organelle called the Endoplasmic Reticulum. Because bacteria lack internal organelles, their ribosomes float freely in the cytoplasm or <b>anchor directly to the inside of the plasma membrane</b>. This positioning allows them to immediately pump newly made proteins out of the cell or straight into the membrane where they are needed.

#### 2. Size and Subunits (The 70S Puzzle):

<b>The Two Halves:</b> A ribosome is shaped a bit like a hamburger bun, made of a <b>Large Subunit (50S)</b> and a <b>Small Subunit (30S)</b>.

<callout icon="💡" color="gray_bg">
<b>Why 50 + 30 = 70?</b> The "<b>S</b>" stands for <b>Svedberg units</b>, which measures how fast a particle settles to the bottom of a tube when spun at extreme speeds in a centrifuge. It measures <i>density and shape</i>, not weight. When the 50S and 30S pieces lock together to do work, they fit tightly, changing their shape and density. As a combined unit, they sediment at a rate of <b>70S</b>.
</callout>

#### 3. Function: Protein Synthesis:

The main job of a ribosome is translation. It reads a ribbon of genetic code called <b>mRNA</b> (messenger RNA) and translates that code into a chain of amino acids, creating a <b>protein</b>.

#### 4. Efficiency Boost: Polysomes (The Factory Chain):

Bacteria need to grow and multiply incredibly fast. Waiting for one ribosome to read a strand of mRNA from start to finish before the next one starts would be too slow.

Instead, as soon as the first ribosome moves down the mRNA strand a little bit, a second ribosome hops onto the front end. Then a third, a fourth, and so on.

This chain of multiple ribosomes working on a single mRNA molecule is called a <b>polysome</b> (or polyribosome). It allows a single genetic blueprint to be mass-produced into dozens of identical proteins simultaneously!

## <i><span color="blue">Inclusion Bodies: The Storage Vaults of Bacteria</span></i>

Because bacteria live in unpredictable environments where nutrients can be abundant one day and completely gone the next, they evolved <b>inclusion bodies</b>. These are non-membranous, inorganic or organic aggregates floating freely in the cytoplasm that act as the cell's storage reserves.

Think of them like a bacterium's built-in pantry or emergency battery packs. When food is plentiful, the cell packs extra nutrients away into these dense droplets so it doesn't starve later.

<b>Major Types of Inclusion Bodies:</b>

- <b>Phosphate Granules (Volutin/Metachromatic Granules):</b> These store large reserves of inorganic phosphate. Bacteria use these reserves to quickly generate <b>ATP (energy)</b> and build new DNA molecules.
- <b>Glycogen Granules:</b> These store dense chains of carbohydrates. They serve as a direct, easily accessible <b>carbon and energy source</b> when the bacterium runs out of external food.
- <b>Cyanophycean Granules:</b> Unique to cyanobacteria, these dense bodies store polymers of amino acids (proteins), acting as a massive <b>nitrogen reserve</b> to help the cell survive periods of nutrient starvation.
- <b>Gas Vacuoles:</b> Found in aquatic bacteria (like blue-green, green and purple photosynthetic bacteria). These are not solid food stores; instead, they are hollow, protein-skinned cylinders that trap air. They function exactly like a submarine's ballast tanks, letting the bacteria <b>float up or down</b> to find the perfect level of sunlight for photosynthesis.

<callout icon="💡" color="gray_bg">
<b>Note: </b>Most of the bacteria store Phosphate Granules and  Glycogen Granules,<b> </b>but there are some exceptional bacteria that do not store these granules at all, even when nutrients are abundant.

In the bacterial world, inclusion bodies like phosphate and glycogen granules are evolutionary adaptations for survival in highly unstable environments (like soil, water, or a host's gut). However, certain bacteria have adapted to live in ways that make these storage vaults completely unnecessary.
</callout>

#### The primary groups of bacteria that lack these storage structures include:

<b>1. Obligate Intracellular Parasites (The Ultimate Freeloaders): </b>Some bacteria can only survive <i>inside</i> the cells of a host. Because they are constantly bathed in a rich, stable soup of nutrients provided by the host cell, they have completely lost the genetic machinery required to make and store food reserves.

- <b><u>Chlamydia</u></b><b>:</b> Famous obligate intracellular pathogens. Because they live entirely within host cells, they have extremely stripped-down genomes and do not produce inclusion bodies for nutrient storage.
- <b><u>Rickettsia</u></b><b>:</b> The bacteria responsible for diseases like typhus. They live inside host cytoplasm, stealing energy directly from the host, meaning they never face starvation and have no need for phosphate or glycogen granules.

<b>2. Bacteria with Ultra-Minimized Genomes: </b>Through a process called reductive evolution, certain bacteria have discarded any gene that isn't absolutely essential for basic survival.

- <b><u>Mycoplasma</u></b><b>:</b> These are the smallest free-living organisms on Earth. They lack a cell wall and have a tiny genome. Because they have shed all non-essential genetic weight, they do not possess the metabolic pathways to synthesize or pack large storage aggregates like glycogen.

<b>3. Structural Exceptions (Alternative Storage): </b>Some bacteria do not store <i>glycogen</i> because they rely entirely on an entirely different biochemical pathway for carbon:

- Many soil and industrial bacteria (like certain species of <i><u>Pseudomonas</u></i> or <i><u>Azotobacter</u></i>) do not make glycogen granules at all. Instead, they choose to convert excess carbon into <b>PHB (poly-β-hydroxybutyrate)</b>, which forms distinct, lipid-like inclusion bodies instead.
